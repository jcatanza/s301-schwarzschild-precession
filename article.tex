\begin{abstract}
S301 orbits Sgr~A$^*$ with $e = 0.9832$ and $P = 8.68$~yr, reaching 136 Schwarzschild radii and more than 8\% of $c$ at periapsis \citep{gravity2026}. Its orbit must show Schwarzschild (first-post-Newtonian) apsidal precession, the effect that shifts Mercury's perihelion, independent of the black hole's spin. We ask whether a near-term, ground-based, astrometry-only GRAVITY+ campaign spanning the 2031.8 and 2040.5 passages can detect it. We validate a relativistic light-curve model against the published solution, design a \campaignNEpochs{}-epoch cadence by Fisher-information optimization with a periapsis-coverage floor, fit a seven-parameter orbit that includes light-travel-time delay to simulated astrometry, and build a systematic error budget anchored to published instrumental figures. The fit recovers the injected precession rate, \fitTruthOmegaDotDegYr{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$, as $\fitFitOmegaDotDegYr \pm \fitSigmaOmegaDotDegYr$~deg~yr$^{-1}$ at the discovery paper's forecast precision of 100~$\mu$as per epoch, or $\pm\precisionAchievedOmegaDotSigma$ at the 207~$\mu$as achieved on S301 to date. Marginalizing over the Sgr~A$^*$ reference zero point and the mass and distance priors, and adding the bounded systematics (extended mass, lensing, source confusion) in quadrature, the realistic precision is \budgetBottomLine{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$. A radial-velocity channel adds nothing measurable at S301's magnitude; a photometric channel adds nothing to the fit but independently validates the light-curve physics. The first periapsis falls in solar conjunction and is unobservable by any facility for \conjunctionGapTotalDays{}~days; we quantify what that costs. Sgr~A$^*$'s unknown spin could shift the measured rate by \spinLtPctChiNineZero{}--\spinLtPctChiOneZeroZero{}\%, which is why this is a spin-agnostic rather than a spin-free test. This is a synthetic forecast; confirmation awaits the passages themselves.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Galactic Center's S-stars have delivered some of the strongest strong-field tests of general relativity to date, including gravitational redshift and Schwarzschild precession in the orbit of S2 \citep{gravity2018,gravity2020}. S301 \citep{gravity2026} extends this program. It has a shorter period than S2 (8.68~yr vs.\ $\sim$16~yr), a higher eccentricity, and a closer periapsis (136~$R_S$ vs.\ $\sim$1400~$R_S$). S301 was discovered as a spin (Lense--Thirring) probe for Sgr~A$^*$; a companion paper \citep{piran2026} develops the methodology for that harder measurement.

Before a spin measurement, a more basic question needs its own answer. Can a real, currently executable observing campaign detect the ordinary, spin-independent apsidal precession that any Schwarzschild black hole already produces? We answer it by building the complete pipeline a real observing proposal would need: validate the physics model, design the observing campaign, fit the orbit, and account for systematics. Every number quoted below is generated by the accompanying code and inserted into this manuscript by macro, so the text cannot drift from the calculation.

\section{The S301 Orbital Solution}
\label{sec:solution}

All ground-truth parameters are taken from \citet{gravity2026}, Extended Data Table~2, Solution~1 (Table~\ref{tab:params}); a second, nearly degenerate solution is also published but not used here. The discovery paper's own orbit fit includes both the R{\o}mer (light-travel-time) effect and Schwarzschild precession, so these elements are defined in the same relativistic model we use. At periapsis S301 passes 136~$R_S$ from Sgr~A$^*$ at $>$8\%~$c$ ($\sim$25{,}000~km~s$^{-1}$), closer and faster than any other known star. Its effective temperature is not published; we adopt \lightcurveTEff{}~K from standard F1.5V tables, which affects only the light-curve amplitude in Section~\ref{sec:lightcurve}, not the orbital dynamics. The mass and distance of Sgr~A$^*$ are those of \citet{gravity2022massdist}, $M_\bullet = (4.297\pm0.012)\times10^6\,M_\odot$ and $R_0 = 8277\pm9$~pc (statistical), with systematic uncertainties of $\approx40{,}000\,M_\odot$ and $\approx30$~pc.

\section{Simulation Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

\subsection{Relativistic Light Curve}
\label{sec:lightcurve}

We model S301's $K$-band light curve from a full Keplerian solution. The model combines the exact special-relativistic Doppler factor (not a small-$v$ approximation) with the Schwarzschild redshift $\sqrt{1-R_S/r}$, applies the combined shift to a \lightcurveTEff{}~K blackbody through the relativistic-beaming relation $I_\nu/\nu^3$, and integrates the result over a top-hat transmission spanning GRAVITY's real $K$-band operating range on the VLTI (Very Large Telescope Interferometer), \lightcurveBandLoUm{}--\lightcurveBandHiUm{}~$\mu$m \citep{gravity2017}. Three effects are deliberately left out of this curve. Gravitational lensing by Sgr~A$^*$ is not applied to the model, but its astrometric effect is quantified in Section~\ref{sec:lensing} and found negligible for this campaign. Occultation does not apply: nothing physically blocks S301's light along our line of sight, since Sgr~A$^*$ is a compact source rather than an extended eclipsing body. Kerr (spin-dependent) terms are excluded for the same reason spin is excluded everywhere else in this paper: including them would require assuming a value for Sgr~A$^*$'s unmeasured spin. R{\o}mer delay does not affect a curve plotted against time from periapsis; it is part of the campaign and fitting pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:fit}). None of these effects has a counterpart in \citet{piran2026}'s analysis: their method is purely astrometric (multi-star nodal-precession fitting), with no photometric or timing model, so they do not arise there. The model reproduces the published solution: derived $a = \lightcurveAAu{}$~AU vs.\ \lightcurveAAuPublished{}; periapsis \lightcurveRpRs{}~$R_S$ vs.\ \lightcurveRpRsPublished{}; periapsis speed \lightcurveVpPctC{}\%~$c$ vs.\ ``$>$8\%''. This agreement validates the light-curve engine before we use it to predict anything new.

The predicted periapsis signature is not a symmetric absorption dip. No mechanism in this model produces a dip. Instead, the model predicts an \emph{asymmetric} relativistic-beaming spike. As S301 approaches at high velocity, its brightness rises by \lightcurveBrighteningMag{}~mag. As S301 recedes, its brightness falls to \lightcurveDimmingMag{}~mag fainter than baseline, from de-boosting combined with gravitational redshift; redshift dims the star regardless of whether it is approaching or receding. The peak-to-trough amplitude is \lightcurveAmplitudeMag{}~mag (Figure~\ref{fig:lightcurve}). The amplitude is set by the spectral slope in the band: at 2.2~$\mu$m and \lightcurveTEff{}~K, $h\nu/kT\approx0.9$, so the in-band flux scales roughly as $(1+z)^{-1.5}$, not as the bolometric $(1+z)^{-4}$. Near periapsis, the total relativistic redshift exceeds the classical Doppler prediction by \lightcurveRedshiftExcessPpMin{}--\lightcurveRedshiftExcessPpMax{} percentage points, a genuine correction rather than a relabeled classical effect.

The dimming deserves precision, because the intuitive picture---Sgr~A$^*$'s gravity ``pulling away'' some of S301's light near periapsis---is close but not exact. The net fading after periapsis does come from gravity acting on the light itself: gravitational redshift removes energy from each photon climbing out of the potential well, on top of the special-relativistic de-boosting from S301's recession. What the model does not include is light bent away from our line of sight or captured outright; Section~\ref{sec:lensing} shows the bending is a $\sim$20~$\mu$as astrometric effect at a handful of epochs and nothing more.

\subsection{Observing Campaign: Instrument and Cadence}
\label{sec:campaign}

The campaign uses GRAVITY+, the VLTI interferometer that made the S301 discovery. GRAVITY+ combines the light of the four 8.2~m Unit Telescopes at Paranal, with adaptive optics on each, into fringes on baselines up to $\sim$130~m; that baseline, not any single aperture, is what gives it sub-milliarcsecond astrometry in the crowded Galactic Center field. We adopt a per-epoch astrometric precision of \campaignAstrometryUas{}~$\mu$as. This is the value the discovery paper itself adopts for its GRAVITY+ forecast, ``as expected for the final performance of GRAVITY+'' \citep{gravity2026}; it is a forecast, not a demonstrated S301 figure. The precision achieved on S301 in the discovery data is $\approx$207~$\mu$as, set by the pixel grid of their image reconstruction \citep[Methods]{gravity2026}. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the result at both values. GRAVITY+ also delivers $K$-band photometry at every epoch, which we simulate with S301's published uncertainty of \campaignSigmaDmag{}~mag.

\textbf{Radial velocity was considered and rejected before any data was simulated.} SINFONI, an integral-field spectrograph formerly at the VLT (Very Large Telescope), delivered a real, achieved S2 radial-velocity precision of 12.3~km~s$^{-1}$ at $m_K=14.0$ \citep{gravity2018}. ERIS (the Enhanced Resolution Imager and Spectrograph), its successor, houses SPIFFIER, a near-infrared integral-field spectrograph with resolving power $R=5000$ \citep{davies2023}. At S301's magnitude ($\Delta m=\campaignRvDeltaM{}$ fainter than S2, $\sim$\campaignRvFluxRatio{}$\times$ less flux) and in the background-limited regime appropriate to faint Galactic Center spectroscopy, the SINFONI benchmark scales to $\sim$\campaignRvSigmaEris{}~km~s$^{-1}$ per epoch. Two separate ratios matter. First, precision against signal: S301's line-of-sight velocity swings by $\pm$\campaignRvSignalMax{}~km~s$^{-1}$, so the per-epoch noise is \campaignRvPrecisionToSignal{} of the full signal, roughly a tenth. Second, precision against SPIFFIER's own \campaignRvResolutionElement{}~km~s$^{-1}$ resolution element: the ratio is \campaignRvImpliedSnr{}, the fraction of one resolution element the line centroid could be located to. This second ratio is the one a Time Allocation Committee asks about; a value this far below one means the line cannot be centroided at all. The discovery team reports the same conclusion from the telescope: ``Our current ERIS spectroscopy is not deep enough'' \citep{gravity2026}. We confirmed that the loss is immaterial rather than assuming it (Section~\ref{sec:rv}): adding a simulated ERIS channel to the fit changes no parameter's precision. The discovery paper expects future MICADO spectroscopy at the ELT (Extremely Large Telescope) to measure S301's velocity; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to that.

\textbf{Cadence.} S301 spends nearly all of its 8.68~yr period near apoapsis; almost all of its angular motion happens within days of periapsis. Where the epochs land in time therefore strongly determines what the fit can recover. We compared four designs at a fixed budget of \campaignNEpochs{} epochs, all restricted to Sgr~A$^*$'s real Paranal visibility season ($\sim$March--September; \citealt{gravity2026}):

\emph{Design A (heuristic)}: the obvious first guess. About 90\% of epochs within $\pm$\campaignDenseHalfWidthDays{}~days of each periapsis, the rest sparse.

\emph{Design B (D-optimal)}: let the statistics choose. Fisher information measures how much a candidate epoch is predicted to shrink the joint uncertainty on the seven fitted parameters; a D-optimal design picks the epoch set that minimizes the volume of the joint uncertainty ellipsoid (the determinant of the covariance), evaluated at one assumed-true parameter vector and built greedily from a daily, visibility-filtered candidate grid.

\emph{Design C (pseudo-Bayesian D-optimal)}: the same, but averaging the Fisher information over 300 draws from the published element uncertainties rather than linearizing at one point \citep{chaloner1995}, so an epoch that is informative only if the orbit is already known exactly is discounted.

\emph{Design D (constrained)}: Design C's search, but with a hard floor reserving \designFloorFractionRequested{} of the budget for the $\pm$\campaignDenseHalfWidthDays{}-day windows around both periapses before the search runs; after the visibility season culls roughly half of passage~1's window, \designFloorFractionRealized{} of the budget survives in those windows.

Table~\ref{tab:cadence} gives each design's bootstrap precision, the analytic Cram\'er--Rao bound (CRB) evaluated at the truth, and how each distributes its epochs. Design~A is the outlier: its $\dot\omega$ precision is \designBootRatioOmegaDotDOverA{}$\times$ worse than Design~D's, because concentrating 90\% of the budget at periapsis leaves too little angular diversity to pin down the orbit's orientation. Designs~B, C and~D perform alike on $\dot\omega$ (to within bootstrap noise) and all place a substantial fraction of epochs near periapsis on their own (\designNDenseB{}, \designNDenseC{} and \designNDenseD{} of \campaignNEpochs{}). We adopt Design~D. Its floor guarantees periapsis coverage regardless of how the Fisher search behaves, and Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that complete periapsis coverage is what tightens the period and epoch of periapsis; the Fisher-optimal remainder then places the flexible epochs where they resolve the orientation degeneracies. For Design~D the CRB and the bootstrap agree (CRB/bootstrap = \designCrbOverBootOmegaDotD{} for $\dot\omega$), which is the strongest available evidence that the bootstrap uncertainties are trustworthy.

An earlier version of this code, without the visibility-season filter on the candidate grid and with an unbracketed Kepler solver, produced a very different Design~B: the search piled epochs at the campaign's temporal extremes, skipped periapsis, and its Cram\'er--Rao promise was contradicted by a much worse bootstrap. That behavior is a documented pitfall of an unfiltered candidate grid on a nonlinear periodic problem, not a property of Fisher design as such, and it does not occur in the current code; we record it here because the periapsis floor was originally motivated by it and we retain the floor for robustness, not because Designs~B and~C fail.

This single-star cadence problem is a different optimization from \citet{piran2026}'s multi-star differential design, which must additionally synchronize observations across several stars to subtract Newtonian confusion when isolating the spin signal. Here, with one star and one observable, the constraint that matters is periapsis coverage in time, not cross-star simultaneity.

\textbf{Solar conjunction.} The visibility filter exposes a real scheduling gap, and it is more severe than a simple anchoring adjustment. Sgr~A$^*$'s solar elongation drops below $45^\circ$, effectively unobservable by any facility, from \conjunctionElongBelowFourFiveStart{} to \conjunctionElongBelowFourFiveEnd{}, reaching \conjunctionElongMinDeg{}$^\circ$ on \conjunctionElongMinDate{}. The first periapsis (\conjunctionPeriOneDate{}, elongation \conjunctionPeriOneElongDeg{}$^\circ$) falls inside this conjunction. The campaign's last observable epoch before it is \conjunctionGapDaysBeforePeri{}~days before periapsis and the first afterward is \conjunctionGapDaysAfterPeri{}~days after, a \conjunctionGapTotalDays{}-day blackout spanning the periapsis itself and the first \conjunctionGapDaysAfterPeri{}~days of the recovery. This is a hard limit of viewing Sgr~A$^*$ near solar conjunction, not a cadence choice, and it is not specific to Paranal or to astrometry: the elongation stays below $85^\circ$, JWST's own sunshield-driven minimum, from \conjunctionElongBelowEightFiveStart{} to \conjunctionElongBelowEightFiveEnd{}, so a telescope at Earth--Sun L2 gains nothing; HARMONI's site (Cerro Armazones) is 20~km from Paranal and shares the identical sky; and the constraint is geometric, so a spectrograph is no more able to see through it than an imager. The second periapsis falls at elongation \conjunctionPeriTwoElongDeg{}$^\circ$, comfortably in season. Which passage gets the bad draw is calendar arithmetic, not design: with $P = 8.68$~yr, each successive periapsis falls \conjunctionCalendarDriftYr{}~yr later in the calendar than the last. Section~\ref{sec:results} quantifies what the truncated first passage costs.

\subsection{Photometric Cross-Check}
\label{sec:photometry}

Every astrometric epoch also yields a $K$-band brightness, at no extra cost. We generate this photometry from the same band-integrated model as Section~\ref{sec:lightcurve}, but we do not fit it, for a reason we tested rather than asserted. Astrometry alone already determines the orbital state at every epoch through the same seven parameters; photometry is a fixed function of that state and adds no free parameter. It is not zero information---two independent noisy measurements of the same state generally combine---but at S301's photometric uncertainty (\campaignSigmaDmag{}~mag against a \photcheckModelAmplitudeAtEpochs{}~mag modulation) the contribution is negligible: refitting with the photometric channel added leaves $\dot\omega$'s bootstrap precision at \photcheckJointRatioOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$ the astrometry-only value, and every other parameter within bootstrap noise (Section~\ref{sec:rv} reports the analogous radial-velocity test).

Photometry earns its place as an independent validation instead. We take the orbital solution from the astrometry-only fit (Table~\ref{tab:finalfit}), predict the brightness curve from Section~\ref{sec:lightcurve}'s physics, and compare that prediction with the simulated photometry the astrometric fit never saw. The result is $\chi^2 = \photcheckChiTwo{}$ for \photcheckNEpochs{} epochs (reduced $\chi^2 = \photcheckChiTwoReduced{}$, \photcheckChiTwoNsigmaFromExpected{}$\sigma$ from its expectation): an orbit derived purely from positions correctly predicts a brightness measurement it was not fit to. Figure~\ref{fig:fitorbit}'s bottom panel shows why this must remain a statistical rather than a visual check: the per-epoch scatter exceeds the whole signal, and the brightening spike is not detectable by eye in this dataset.

\subsection{Orbit Fit}
\label{sec:fit}

We fit seven parameters to the astrometry: the six Keplerian elements plus the apsidal precession rate $\dot\omega$, applied as $\omega_{\rm eff}(t) = \omega + \dot\omega\,(t - t_{\rm ref})$. The model evaluates the orbit at each epoch's self-consistent emission time, solved by Newton iteration from the arrival time, so the R{\o}mer delay is part of the model on both the truth and the fit side, as in the discovery paper's own analysis. The fit uses Levenberg--Marquardt least squares, starting from the published discovery-paper solution rather than a blind search: a real follow-up would refine the known preliminary orbit, not rediscover it. We perturb the published values by 3\% in period, 0.03 in eccentricity, $10^\circ$ in inclination, $15^\circ$ in node, $12^\circ$ in argument of periapsis, 0.05~yr in periapsis epoch, and 0.1~deg~yr$^{-1}$ in the precession rate, to stand in for realistic uncertainty in a preliminary solution. The semi-major axis follows from $(P, GM_\bullet)$ by Kepler's third law with $M_\bullet$ and $R_0$ fixed at their published values; Section~\ref{sec:massdist} tests what floating them costs. Uncertainties come from \fitNBootstrap{} bootstrap resamples of the epochs, cross-checked against the Cram\'er--Rao bound (Table~\ref{tab:cadence}) and against \cadenceMcNRealizations{} independent noise realizations (Section~\ref{sec:results}). We compare the recovered $\dot\omega$ with the analytic Schwarzschild prediction,
\begin{equation}
\dot\omega = \frac{6\pi\,GM_\bullet}{c^2\,a\,(1-e^2)\,P},
\label{eq:precession}
\end{equation}
the Mercury-perihelion formula applied to S301's orbit. We inject no spin (Lense--Thirring) term anywhere: $i$ and $\Omega$ remain static. Sgr~A$^*$'s spin is unmeasured, and assuming a value would substitute a guess for a measurement.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

The final campaign has \campaignNEpochs{} epochs (\campaignEpochMin{}--\campaignEpochMax{}). Of these, \campaignNDense{} (\campaignDensePct{}\%) fall within $\pm$\campaignDenseHalfWidthDays{}~days of a periapsis, above Design~D's realized floor: the Fisher search placed some of its own flexible budget near periapsis too. The remaining \campaignNSparse{} epochs (\campaignSparsePct{}\%) are not spread evenly across the \campaignBaselineYr{}~yr baseline. They land in a few tight clusters separated by multi-year gaps (Figure~\ref{fig:fitorbit}), including clusters in 2033 and 2037--2038 rather than immediately after passage~1 once conjunction releases the field in 2032. This looks wasteful, and we tested it directly, in two ways.

First, does the conjunction gap itself cost anything? We built a hypothetical design identical to Design~D except that passage~1's dense cluster is a complete, symmetric $\pm$\campaignDenseHalfWidthDays{}-day window centered on true periapsis, as if conjunction did not truncate it, and bootstrapped both. Completing the passage tightens $P$ and $t_0$ to \cadenceCompleteRatioCapPYr{}$\times$ and \cadenceCompleteRatioTPeriYr{}$\times$ their Design~D values and the orientation angles by a few percent (\cadenceCompleteRatioIDeg{}, \cadenceCompleteRatioCapOmegaDeg{}, \cadenceCompleteRatioOmegaDeg{}$\times$), while leaving $\dot\omega$ at \cadenceCompleteRatioOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$, i.e.\ unchanged. So the gap is a real loss for the period and epoch of periapsis and costs nothing measurable on $\dot\omega$, whose precision is set mainly by comparing the orbit's orientation across the $\sim$8.7~yr baseline between the two periapsis-anchored clusters.

Second, given the gap is unavoidable, what should the freed budget do? Moving the \cadenceReallocateNMoved{} flexible epochs into the earliest window the visibility season permits (\cadenceReallocateWindowStart{}--\cadenceReallocateWindowEnd{}, starting \conjunctionGapDaysAfterPeri{}~days after periapsis, well into the slow coast) makes $\dot\omega$'s precision \cadenceReallocateRatio{}$\times$ Design~D's, i.e.\ \cadenceReallocatePctChange{}\% worse, and every placement tried between 2032 and 2037 followed the same pattern (best at \cadenceScanECenter{}: \cadenceScanESigmaOmegaDot{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$; worst at \cadenceScanCCenter{}: \cadenceScanCSigmaOmegaDot{}). Epochs placed right after passage~1 sample a state dynamically close to what the existing dense cluster already measures; epochs at a genuinely different orbital phase resolve the remaining degeneracies (period against periapsis epoch, node against inclination) that would otherwise blur the comparison between the two anchors. The bootstrap uncertainties themselves are corroborated by \cadenceMcNRealizations{} independent noise realizations: the realization-to-realization scatter of $\dot\omega$ is \cadenceMcOverBootOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$ the bootstrap $\sigma$.

Every parameter recovers within \fitPullCapOmegaDeg{}$\sigma$ of truth or better. The fitted sky-plane track (Figure~\ref{fig:fitorbit}, top) shows the periapsis direction rotated between passages, the geometric signature of precession. Several uncertainties in Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} are far tighter than the published ones (e.g.\ $\fitSigmaCapOmegaDeg^\circ$ vs.\ $3.5^\circ$ on $\Omega$). Three idealizations drive this: the campaign assumes the forecast per-epoch precision at every epoch, it spans two full passages the real 2026 dataset does not yet have, and it holds $M_\bullet$ and $R_0$ fixed, which pins the orbit's angular scale (Section~\ref{sec:massdist}). None of this is a claim of outperforming the discovery.

\textbf{Sensitivity to the assumed precision.} At the achieved rather than forecast per-epoch precision (\precisionAchievedUas{}~$\mu$as instead of \precisionForecastUas{}~$\mu$as), the same campaign gives $\dot\omega = \precisionAchievedOmegaDot \pm \precisionAchievedOmegaDotSigma$~deg~yr$^{-1}$, a \precisionAchievedDetectionNsigma{}$\sigma$ detection instead of \precisionForecastDetectionNsigma{}$\sigma$; the orientation angles degrade in proportion ($\sigma_\Omega = \precisionAchievedSigmaCapOmegaDeg^\circ$). The precision scales linearly with the per-epoch error, so a reader can rescale Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} to whatever GRAVITY+ delivers in 2031.

\textbf{A non-circular consistency check.} $\dot\omega$ in Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} is a free seventh parameter, fit from geometry with no reference to Equation~\ref{eq:precession}. For each bootstrap resample we instead compute what that formula predicts from \emph{that resample's own} fitted $(P,e)$, still blind to any published or injected value. The prediction is $\fitPnFromFitMean \pm \fitPnFromFitSigma$~deg~yr$^{-1}$; its per-resample difference from the independently fit $\dot\omega$ is $\fitPnDiffMean \pm \fitPnDiffSigma$~deg~yr$^{-1}$ (\fitPnDiffNsigma{}$\sigma$ from zero, using the per-resample difference because $P$, $e$ and $\dot\omega$ come from the same correlated resamples). Geometry and theory agree without either referencing the other's answer.

\section{Systematic Error Budget}
\label{sec:budget}

The uncertainties in Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} are random (photon-noise-driven) only. Each systematic below is anchored to a published instrumental or astrophysical figure, injected into the synthetic data, and refit with the naive model to measure its bias; where a correction exists, we also measure what the correction costs in precision. Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget} collects the terms; Section~\ref{sec:bottomline} combines them.

\subsection{Extended-Mass (Newtonian) Confusion}
\label{sec:extmass}

Any undetected extended mass near Sgr~A$^*$ retrogradely shifts the periapsis and biases the apsidal precession low, an effect first modeled quantitatively by \citet{rubilar2001} for S2-like orbits. We reproduce their post-Newtonian treatment exactly: a spherically symmetric distribution combining the point mass with a Plummer component ($\alpha=5$), integrated through two periapsis passages via their Eq.~A.7 equation of motion, measuring the eccentricity-vector rotation between them. The point-mass-only case validates the integrator against Equation~\ref{eq:precession} to \extmassPointmassAgreementPct{}\%, consistent with the $(v/c)^2\approx0.7\%$ second-post-Newtonian scale at S301's periapsis speed; because the extended-mass bias is a difference between two runs of the same integrator, this common-mode residual mostly cancels. For the extended-mass magnitude and core radius we use \citet{gravity2022massdist}'s own multi-star constraint self-consistently: their Plummer scale length ($0.3''\approx\extmassRcMpc{}$~mpc) with their mass bound of $\lesssim$\extmassMExtOneMsun{}~$M_\odot$ (1$\sigma$) or $\lesssim$\extmassMExtThreeMsun{}~$M_\odot$ (3$\sigma$). The resulting bias on $\dot\omega$ is \extmassBiasRateOne{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$ at the 1$\sigma$ bound (\extmassBiasPctOne{}\% of the signal, \extmassBiasNsigmaOne{}$\sigma$ of Table~\ref{tab:finalfit}'s precision) and \extmassBiasRateThree{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$ at the 3$\sigma$ bound (\extmassBiasPctThree{}\%, \extmassBiasNsigmaThree{}$\sigma$). S301's periapsis (\extmassRpMpc{}~mpc) sits so far inside any plausible core radius that only a small fraction of the already-small bounded mass is enclosed there. This estimate is narrower than \citet{piran2026}'s: they model a flattened disk's nodal precession competing with the spin signal across multiple stars; we model a spherical distribution's apsidal effect on S301 alone. The two are not directly comparable, but both find S301 comparatively well protected (their Section~3 reports S301's disk/LT confusion ratio $\sim$10$\times$ better than S2's).

\textbf{Discrete perturbers.} A smooth profile is not the whole story. The discovery paper's dynamical analysis notes that at S301's radius, angular-momentum and energy perturbations from a handful of massive perturbers have heavy-tailed statistics, so orbital evolution ``would be dominated by large, non-local jumps'' \citep[Methods]{gravity2026}, and gives relaxation and collision timescales of $\gtrsim 10^7$~yr against a Schwarzschild precession timescale of $1.6\times10^3$~yr. Over the two passages of this campaign the expected stochastic perturbation to $\omega$ is therefore several orders of magnitude below the precession signal, but a single close encounter is a rare, unbounded event that no smooth-profile budget captures. We flag it rather than model it; \citet{piran2026} treat the multi-star version of this concern.

\subsection{Astrometric Reference: the Sgr~A$^*$ Zero Point}
\label{sec:refframe}

The leading instrumental systematic for the S2 precession detection was the reference-frame tie: the adaptive-optics imaging (NACO) frame had to be registered to the radio frame through a zero point and drift, and \citet{gravity2020} identify those frame parameters as ``the limiting factor'' of that measurement. That systematic does not apply here, because GRAVITY+ does not use the NACO frame. The frame parameters of \citet{gravity2020} are defined for the NACO data, ``relative to the AO spectroscopic or imaging frames''; GRAVITY's own positions ``are directly referring to Sgr~A$^*$, since it is visible in each exposure and since it is one of the point sources in the multi-source model'' \citep{gravity2022massdist}, and the S301 discovery fit accordingly allows ``no coordinate system offsets, as our data are interferometrically referenced directly to the near-infrared counterpart of Sgr~A$^*$'' \citep{gravity2026}. An earlier draft of this paper injected the NACO priors of \citet{plewa2015} and found them dominant; that analysis has been removed as inapplicable to this instrument.

What can go wrong for interferometric referencing is different in kind. First, a constant offset between Sgr~A$^*$'s near-infrared photocentre (the accretion flow and its flares) and the mass centroid the orbit is bound to. It is bounded physically by the emitting region's size, a few Schwarzschild radii ($1\,R_S = 10~\mu$as; EHT ring diameter $51.8~\mu$as, \citealt{eht2022}); we adopt a \refframeOffsetPriorUas{}~$\mu$as prior with \refframeOffsetPriorUasConservative{}~$\mu$as as the conservative case. Being common to every epoch it is absorbed exactly by two extra parameters $(x_0,y_0)$, but it biases the seven-parameter model, which assumes the focus sits at the origin. Second, per-epoch reference jitter as the photocentre wanders; GRAVITY's quoted $\sim$65--100~$\mu$as S2-minus-Sgr~A$^*$ precision \citep{gravity2020} already includes this, so it is largely inside the campaign's \campaignAstrometryUas{}~$\mu$as floor, and we scan an additional 50 and 100~$\mu$as in quadrature as a sensitivity.

Injecting a one-prior-sigma offset (\refframeInjectedOffsetComponentFid{}~$\mu$as in each coordinate) and refitting the seven-parameter model biases $\dot\omega$ by \refframeNaiveBiasFid{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$ (\refframeNaiveBiasNsigmaFid{}$\sigma$, \refframeNaiveBiasPctFid{}\% of the signal); the conservative offset gives \refframeNaiveBiasNsigmaCons{}$\sigma$ (\refframeNaiveBiasPctCons{}\%). Fitting $(x_0,y_0)$ jointly with the prior recovers the injected offset (\refframeJointOffsetXFid{}, \refframeJointOffsetYFid{}~$\mu$as vs.\ \refframeInjectedOffsetComponentFid{}), leaves a $\dot\omega$ residual of \refframeJointResidualPctFid{}\%, and costs \refframeJointSigmaRatioFid{}$\times$ in bootstrap precision ($\sigma = \refframeJointSigmaFid{}$~deg~yr$^{-1}$). Reference jitter of 50 or 100~$\mu$as changes $\dot\omega$'s precision to \refframeJitterFiveZeroRatio{}$\times$ and \refframeJitterOneZeroZeroRatio{}$\times$ the baseline. The reference systematic that actually applies to this campaign is therefore sub-$\sigma$ even if ignored and essentially free once modeled.

\subsection{Source Confusion}
\label{sec:confusion}

Any neighboring star within roughly one diffraction-limited beam blends into the same single-mode-fiber output and biases the recovered photocentre by its flux-weighted contribution. We bound this with a Monte Carlo of \confusionNMc{} field realizations around S301 ($m_K=19.3$), drawing neighbor counts from \citet{gravity2022massdist}'s quoted surface density ($>$100 stars per square arcsecond to $K<17$, with the number--magnitude slope to $K=21$ from \citeauthor{rubilar2001}'s quoted Jaroszy\'nski 1999 ratio), weighted by a Gaussian beam matched to \citet{gravity2020}'s \confusionBeamFwhmMas{}~mas single-UT beam. Applied naively, as a single-aperture photocentre, the median bias is \confusionNaiveMedianMas{}~mas per epoch, wildly larger than GRAVITY's demonstrated precision, confirming that the naive number is the wrong quantity. \citet{gravity2022massdist} state interferometric astrometry is ``much less affected (by a factor of several hundred)'' by confusion; bounding that as \confusionSuppressionLow{}--\confusionSuppressionHigh{}$\times$, the 90th-percentile confusion floor is \confusionFloorPNineZeroHighUas{}--\confusionFloorPNineZeroLowUas{}~$\mu$as, comparable to or below the photon-noise floor. Because neighboring stars move on their own orbits, confusion is per-epoch scatter rather than a coherent drift; propagated as such through the bootstrap at the conservative end, it changes $\dot\omega$'s precision by a factor \confusionSigmaRatio{} and shifts the best fit by \confusionBestFitShift{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Gravitational Lensing}
\label{sec:lensing}

Sgr~A$^*$ bends S301's light when the star is on the far side of the black hole. For a point lens the primary image of a source at separation $\theta_s$ sits at $\theta_+ = [\theta_s + (\theta_s^2 + 4\theta_E^2)^{1/2}]/2$ with $\theta_E^2 = (4GM_\bullet/c^2)\,D_{LS}/(D_L D_S)$, where $D_{LS}$ is the star's depth behind the lens. We apply the exact shift epoch by epoch to the noise-free astrometry and refit. Only \lensingNFarSide{} of the \campaignNEpochs{} epochs are on the far side; the largest shift is \lensingMaxShiftUas{}~$\mu$as ($\theta_E = \lensingThetaEAtMaxUas{}$~$\mu$as) at the far-side periapsis of passage~2, \lensingNShiftGtOneZerouas{} epoch exceeds 10~$\mu$as, and the RMS shift over the campaign is \lensingRmsShiftUas{}~$\mu$as. The lens-unaware fit is biased by \lensingOmegaDotBias{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$ in $\dot\omega$ (\lensingOmegaDotBiasNsigma{}$\sigma$) and by at most \lensingMaxAngleBiasNsigma{}$\sigma$ in any orientation angle. Lensing is real, computable, and negligible for this campaign; we include it in Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget} rather than leave it as an unquantified limitation.

\subsection{R{\o}mer (Light-Travel-Time) Delay}
\label{sec:roemer}

An observation epoch is an arrival time; the light left the star earlier by the light-travel time to its line-of-sight position, which varies over the orbit. S301's orbital diameter implies a maximum swing of $2a/c \approx \roemerMaxSwingDays{}$~days, comparable to the $\pm$\campaignDenseHalfWidthDays{}-day periapsis windows; across the campaign epochs the correction ranges from \roemerLtMinDays{} to \roemerLtMaxDays{}~days (RMS \roemerLtRmsDays{}~days). The pipeline includes it (Section~\ref{sec:fit}), so Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} is unaffected. Ignoring it would not be harmless: fitting light-time-correct, noise-free data with a light-time-unaware model biases $\dot\omega$ by \roemerNaiveOmegaDotBias{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$ (\roemerNaiveOmegaDotBiasPct{}\% of the signal, \roemerNaiveOmegaDotBiasNsigma{}$\sigma$) and the orientation angles by \roemerNaiveIBias{}$^\circ$, \roemerNaiveCapOmegaBias{}$^\circ$ and \roemerNaiveOmegaBias{}$^\circ$ in $i$, $\Omega$ and $\omega$ (\roemerNaiveAngleNsigmaMin{}--\roemerNaiveAngleNsigmaMax{}$\sigma$). The correction is a deterministic function of the orbit already being fit, so it adds no free parameters: the light-time-aware model reproduces the noise-free data to machine precision ($\roemerAwareOmegaDotResidual$~deg~yr$^{-1}$ residual), and on noisy data its bootstrap precision is \roemerSigmaRatio{}$\times$ the unaware model's, i.e.\ the correction is free in precision as well as exact in bias. Making the correction converge robustly surfaced a genuine bug in the project's Kepler-equation solver: undamped Newton--Raphson from $E_0 = M$ diverges permanently for ordinary mean anomalies at $e=0.9832$ (verified at $M=0.2185$~rad, where the iterate reaches $E\sim10^{13}$). The solver is now a bisection-safeguarded Newton iteration, verified against $1.4\times10^6$ random $(M,e)$ pairs to $e=0.9999$ with worst-case residual $2\times10^{-12}$, and every result in this paper is computed with it.

\subsection{Mass and Distance Priors}
\label{sec:massdist}

Table~\ref{tab:finalfit} holds $M_\bullet$ and $R_0$ fixed, which pins the orbit's angular scale ($a/R_0$ with $a^3 = GM_\bullet P^2/4\pi^2$) by the period alone. Real S-star fits float both. Astrometry alone constrains only $GM_\bullet/R_0^3$, the classic mass--distance degeneracy that radial velocities normally break, so we refit with $M_\bullet$ and $R_0$ free under Gaussian priors from \citet{gravity2022massdist} (statistical and systematic combined: \massdistSigmaMPct{}\% and \massdistSigmaRZeroPct{}\%). The precision on $P$ is nearly unchanged (\massdistRatioCapPYr{}$\times$), because the period comes from timing, not scale; $e$, $i$ and $\omega$ widen to \massdistRatioE{}, \massdistRatioIDeg{} and \massdistRatioOmegaDeg{}$\times$ their fixed-scale values, and $\dot\omega$ to \massdistRatioOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$ ($\massdistFitOmegaDot \pm \massdistSigmaOmegaDotDegYr$~deg~yr$^{-1}$, a shift of \massdistOmegaDotShiftNsigma{}$\sigma$). The fixed scale was therefore over-constraining the orbit's shape and orientation, as a referee anticipated, while the precession rate is only modestly affected. The recovered ratios ($M/M_{\rm pub} = \massdistMRatio \pm \massdistMRatioSigma$, $R_0/R_{0,{\rm pub}} = \massdistDRatio \pm \massdistDRatioSigma$) return the priors, as they must for astrometry-only data.

\subsection{Beyond the Budget: Spin (Lense--Thirring) Contamination}
\label{sec:spincontam}

The terms above have a known or boundable magnitude. Sgr~A$^*$'s spin is different in kind. For a spin aligned with S301's orbital angular momentum, frame-dragging produces a purely apsidal shift (\texttt{orbit.lense\_thirring\_apsidal\_rate}, validated against \citeauthor{gravity2026}'s quoted 0.114~deg/orbit at $\chi=1$) that adds directly and indistinguishably to the $\dot\omega$ this pipeline fits, with no upper bound to inject. Using the discovery paper's own fiducial benchmark ($\chi=1$, aligned) and an independent outflow-method measurement \citep[$\chi=0.90\pm0.06$;][]{daly2024}, the possible contamination is \spinLtRateChiNineZero{}--\spinLtRateChiOneZeroZero{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$: \spinLtPctChiNineZero{}--\spinLtPctChiOneZeroZero{}\% of the Schwarzschild signal, \spinLtNsigmaNoiseChiNineZero{}--\spinLtNsigmaNoiseChiOneZeroZero{}$\sigma$ of Table~\ref{tab:finalfit}'s random precision (\spinLtNsigmaRefChiNineZero{}--\spinLtNsigmaRefChiOneZeroZero{}$\sigma$ of the reference-marginalized precision), larger than every bounded systematic after its correction. A measured $\dot\omega$ from S301 alone cannot distinguish a pure point-mass test from GR plus aligned frame-dragging; that is the degeneracy \citet{piran2026}'s multi-star method exists to break.

Rather than multiply the separate precision costs of the reference offset (\refframeJointSigmaRatioFid{}$\times$) and the mass--distance priors (\massdistRatioOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$), which assumes they are independent, we fit all four nuisance parameters jointly with the orbit (11 parameters) and bootstrap: the marginalized precision is \budgetCombinedSigma{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$, \budgetCombinedRatio{}$\times$ the random-only value. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Instrumentation: astrometry.} No operating alternative beats GRAVITY+ for this measurement, and the comparison comes down to resolution. Roman's 2.4~m aperture has a diffraction limit of order 223~mas, larger than S301's entire 83~mas orbit. JWST's (the James Webb Space Telescope's) 6.5~m aperture reaches $\sim$85~mas, comparable to the orbit itself. GRAVITY+'s $\sim$130~m interferometric baseline reaches $\sim$100~$\mu$as. Only the last resolves the orbit's shape, let alone its precession.

\textbf{Instrumentation: radial velocity.}
\label{sec:rv}
Adding a simulated radial-velocity channel to the astrometric fit at ERIS precision (\rvtestErisRvSigma{}~km~s$^{-1}$) changes $\dot\omega$'s precision by \rvtestErisPctChangeOmegaDot{}\%, i.e.\ nothing outside bootstrap noise, which is why no ERIS time is requested. Roman's slitless spectroscopy ($R\approx461$--890) fails independently of integration time because its point spread function is not adaptive-optics-corrected in this crowded field. JWST/NIRSpec has excellent resolution \citep{shajib2025} but reported Galactic-Center-specific operational problems (saturation on bright cluster members, micro-shutter cross-talk, guide-star misidentification; \citealt{yusefzadeh2025}) and no published precision for a source this faint. The ELT is the real prospect, and the discovery team's stated plan is MICADO spectroscopy, which they expect ``will have no problem'' measuring S301's velocity; their spin forecast assumes 1~km~s$^{-1}$ \citep{gravity2026}. We can only partly reconcile that with our own ELT estimate. Extending this paper's validated background-limited scaling to HARMONI's 39~m aperture and $R\approx17{,}385$ K-band modes \citep{harmoni2024} predicts $\sigma_{\rm RV}\approx\rvtestHarmoniRvSigma{}$~km~s$^{-1}$ (signal-to-noise of order \rvtestHarmoniSnr{}), a factor $\sim$20 short of the discovery paper's MICADO figure; neither number rests on an achieved observation, and the MICADO figure is stated without a sensitivity calculation. Either would be genuinely useful: with the HARMONI figure, a radial-velocity channel tightens $P$, $e$ and $t_0$ by tens of percent and the orientation angles by a quarter to a third, but improves $\dot\omega$ by only \rvtestHarmoniPctChangeOmegaDot{}\% (Table~\ref{tab:finalfit}'s two-passage astrometry already constrains the precession rate tightly). On timeline, ESO's schedule has ELT first light in 2029 and HARMONI science operations from late 2030 \citep{eso2026}, so passage~1 (October 2031) is a stretch goal for any ELT instrument and passage~2 (2040.5) is comfortable. Adding a real velocity channel by passage~2 is worth planning a proposal around, chiefly for the mass--distance degeneracy of Section~\ref{sec:massdist}, not for $\dot\omega$ itself.

\textbf{Scope.} This paper's result is a \emph{spin-independent} (spin-agnostic) test of general relativity, and the phrase needs a concrete definition. The quantity measured, $\dot\omega$ in Equation~\ref{eq:precession}, is the Schwarzschild (first-post-Newtonian, monopole-mass) apsidal precession. Any sufficiently compact central mass produces it, whether or not it spins; it is the same effect that advances Mercury's perihelion around the Sun, whose spin plays no role in that calculation. Lense--Thirring precession is different: frame-dragging from Sgr~A$^*$'s spin produces nodal precession and, for an aligned spin, additional apsidal precession too (Section~\ref{sec:spincontam}). \emph{Spin-independent} means the measurement needs no knowledge of the spin to be a valid test; it does not mean spin has no effect on the number reported. If Sgr~A$^*$'s spin is nonzero and aligned, the reported $\dot\omega$ could be biased by up to \spinLtPctChiOneZeroZero{}\%, and that possibility is not resolved here. Measuring the spin is explicitly outside this work, as, for now, is real data: like this paper, \citet{piran2026} is a forecast. This study's contribution to the eventual spin measurement is the part it completes: a validated light-curve and astrometric pipeline, a tested cadence, and the systematic budget of Table~\ref{tab:errorbudget}.

\textbf{Design lesson.} Fisher-information design assumes the model is well approximated by its linear expansion around the assumed parameters. Averaging the Fisher information over a prior on those parameters, the pseudo-Bayesian approach of \citet{chaloner1995} used in Designs~C and~D, is the standard remedy when the assumed values are uncertain, and it costs nothing here. The broader lesson of this project is less about Fisher design than about verification: the most consequential errors we found (a Kepler solver that diverged silently for ordinary inputs, a photometric proxy with twice the amplitude of the validated model, a candidate grid that ignored solar conjunction, and a systematic imported from a different instrument) were each caught only by running a real nonlinear fit or re-reading a primary source, never by the analytic prediction or the first draft. For nonlinear, periodic problems evaluated over multi-cycle baselines, an untested shortcut is not a conservative default.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:summary}

A relativistic light-curve model reproduces S301's published solution to $<$0.1\%. It predicts an asymmetric beaming feature of \lightcurveAmplitudeMag{}~mag, not an occultation dip: Sgr~A$^*$ is a compact source and blocks nothing. The feature is not visually detectable against S301's \campaignSigmaDmag{}~mag photometric uncertainty, so the science result rests on astrometry; the photometry serves instead as an independent check that passes ($\chi^2/N = \photcheckChiTwoReduced{}$).

A radial-velocity channel at ERIS precision adds nothing measurable, in agreement with the discovery team's own finding that ERIS is not deep enough, so the campaign proposes astrometry only. Four cadence designs compared at a fixed \campaignNEpochs{}-epoch budget show that pure Fisher-optimal designs perform as well as the adopted constrained design on $\dot\omega$ and all beat the periapsis-heavy heuristic; the adopted design's periapsis floor is retained for robustness. Solar conjunction makes the first periapsis and the first \conjunctionGapDaysAfterPeri{}~days of its recovery unobservable from anywhere near Earth; completing that passage would tighten $P$ and $t_0$ but not $\dot\omega$.

The astrometry-only, two-passage GRAVITY+ campaign recovers the injected Schwarzschild precession rate (\fitTruthOmegaDotDegYr{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$) as $\fitFitOmegaDotDegYr \pm \fitSigmaOmegaDotDegYr$~deg~yr$^{-1}$ at the forecast precision and $\pm\precisionAchievedOmegaDotSigma$ at the achieved precision. A non-circular check confirms it: the 1PN formula evaluated on the fit's own period and eccentricity agrees with the independently fit rate to \fitPnDiffNsigma{}$\sigma$.

The systematic budget, every term anchored to a published figure, is dominated by no single instrumental effect. Extended-mass confusion biases $\dot\omega$ by at most \extmassBiasNsigmaThree{}$\sigma$; the Sgr~A$^*$ reference offset that applies to interferometric astrometry is sub-$\sigma$ even if ignored and free once fit; source confusion and lensing are negligible; the R{\o}mer delay is in the model and costs nothing; floating $M_\bullet$ and $R_0$ costs \massdistRatioOmegaDotDegYr{}$\times$. Fitting all nuisances jointly and adding the bounded terms gives a realistic precision of \budgetBottomLine{}~deg~yr$^{-1}$, \budgetBottomLinePctOfSignal{}\% of the signal, still random-error-limited. An aligned Sgr~A$^*$ spin at published estimates would shift the rate by \spinLtPctChiNineZero{}--\spinLtPctChiOneZeroZero{}\% with no bound to subtract; that is why this is a spin-agnostic, not spin-free, test.

This is a synthetic forecast, not a completed measurement. Once the 2031.8 and 2040.5 passages are observed, a two-passage GRAVITY+ campaign would be feasible with existing instrumentation and sufficient for an independent, spin-agnostic test of general relativity near Sgr~A$^*$, ahead of the harder spin measurement that motivated S301's discovery.
